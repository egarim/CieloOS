Design notes · not part of the product

Was the bet right?

The brief for this version: the reasons are already good, only the typography is failing them. Keep every word, set it properly, gloss the hard phrases in the margin. Here is what happened when I actually did that.

Half true. The sentences survive being read by a non-engineer once three or four terms are glossed — nothing had to be softened, and no claim had to be dropped. But the paragraph's order is wrong for someone deciding, and typography cannot reorder relevance. Splitting it into lanes is editing, not typesetting. So the honest verdict is: good prose, wrong shape.

Where it held

  • The first sentence of each reason is the best short explanation anyone has written of that danger. “the destination may have been chosen by text Cielo read on a previous page” needs no simplification at all — it just needed to not be sentence one of a wall.
  • Glossing beats rewriting. egress, cross-origin redirect and vision agent each take one sentence of plain English, and the precise term stays on screen, so the person who knows it is not condescended to and the person who doesn't is not lied to.
  • Marginalia keeps the reading line intact. Notes open beside the sentence, not on top of it, and they stay open — no tooltip that vanishes when you look away.

Where it broke

  • Three speech acts in one paragraph. Each reason mixes (a) what the danger is, (b) what your answer covers, (c) what is refused regardless. In the source they are interleaved, and the roadmap sentence sits second — the least decision-relevant line in the most read position. I had to split by hand into lanes.
  • The banned vocabulary made “keep the real text” impossible. The last sentence of the navigation reason names two internal parts of the system by their code names, and both are on this product's never-show list — so they are not repeated here either. What that sentence became on screen: the clause naming the component that does the second, independent refusal now reads “Separately from this question, Cielo refuses…”, and the clause crediting the thing that closes the script-execution door now credits “Cielo's rules”. One more substitution, this one only for plainness: “the per-desk domain allowlist” → “the list of sites already approved for this desk”. So the words on screen are not literally the engineer's words, and the approach's own premise is dented before the first sentence is set.
  • A new sentence had to be written anyway. The three-second read is the headline, and no sentence in the source can be lifted into it — the source opens with “Navigation IS the egress decision”, which is meaningless cold. The compression is authored. Which means this approach is not “only typography” either; it is annotation plus one written line, and that line carries the load for anyone in a hurry.
  • The typing reason has no third lane. It never says what is refused regardless of your answer, because nothing is. The layout makes that hole visible rather than filling it — useful for the writer, thin for the reader.

What I'd watch in testing

  • Whether anyone opens a gloss at all. If nobody clicks, the annotation is decoration and the headline is the whole product.
  • Whether the headline and the first lane saying the same thing twice reads as thorough or as padding.
  • Whether three waiting requests at this length cause people to answer the focused one and stop reading the rest — the failure this approach is most exposed to.

Deliberate refusals

No answer-all, in either direction. No pre-selected button, no colour that makes refusing look like the error, identical size and weight on both. After a decision the next request's buttons stay unarmed for a moment, so a second click does not land on a prompt nobody has read.
