Opening an address is the moment fetching turns into sending: it is where information can leave this machine, and the address may have been chosen by text Cielo read on an earlier page rather than by you. A person confirms it until Cielo can keep a list of hosts you have already trusted, which turns routine browsing back into something Cielo does on its own while keeping the first visit to an unknown host a decision you make.

Your approval binds to the destination, not just to the request. If the address you approved answers by redirecting somewhere else, Cielo refuses and names the new address, so a server cannot substitute a different destination after you have already agreed.

Separately, Cielo will only ever open http and https addresses. Anything else — javascript:, data:, file: — is refused before it reaches you, so a link cannot use this moment to run code.

Sending is the one thing on this machine that cannot be taken back. Everything else Cielo does can be undone, retried or ignored; a message that has left has been read by someone who now believes you wrote it, and who may act on it.

It also carries your name. Cielo composes from what it has read — your calendar, the thread, whatever page was open — and any of that can be wrong or planted. The consequence of a mistake lands on your reputation, not on Cielo’s, so the person whose name is on it decides.

A confirmation is required per message. Cielo does not gain the ability to send further mail by being allowed to send this one.

How this holds up

Notes for the review. None of this appears in the sheet itself.

Three depths, and only the middle one decides

The line is three seconds and always visible. The fork is where the choice is actually made. The full reason is one click and unabridged.

Nobody is asked to decide from the line alone. It exists so that someone facing three at once can tell them apart before opening any of them.

The line compresses; it does not substitute

The test: would someone who read only the line wave through a hostile version of the same action?

“This site will see your IP address” fails — true, and pointed at the wrong danger. Ours names where the address came from, which is the thing actually at stake, and the long reason underneath says the same thing at length.

Both futures cost something

Every fork carries one amber line per side, and the decline side’s cost is written as plainly as the allow side’s — the printer stays broken, Lena keeps waiting.

A design where declining looks free teaches people to decline until something breaks, and then to stop reading altogether.

Green and red are withheld from the choice

The two buttons are identical: same size, same weight, same white, same ink, same border. Neither is focused on open, and Return answers nothing.

Green and red appear only after a decision, recording what happened.

The queue refuses to be efficient

No Approve all. No countdown, no expiry, no badge that reddens. Opening one closes the others, so only one decision is live at a time.

Answering does not auto–advance to the next — the next one is opened by the person, not served to them. Order is arrival order, stated as such, because ranking by risk would be Cielo grading its own homework.

Where this is weakest

A fork is two columns of prose. It reads well once and skims badly on the fiftieth prompt, and the honest cost line on the decline side is the one a hurried person will use as permission to click Allow.

It is also the most expensive format to write: every action type needs four true, weighted statements, and a lazily written pair degrades into filler faster than a single sentence would.

Fraunces for the action, Inter for the reading, system mono for anything shown literally. The sheet keeps its light palette in either theme — it is a picture of the OS, and the OS does not change because you are reading in the dark.
